Mike Cherman & Vic Lee Explain Chinatown Market's Rename to MA®KET

interview2021-09-0800:11:51

Summary

  • Mike and Vic Lee explain the Chinatown Market-to-MARKET rename: the casual original naming, public criticism, internal team process, community history, and the choice to listen before defending.
  • The original name was barely examinedMike says the name emerged quickly from Canal Street references before the brand became consequential.
  • Intent did not erase the problemHe acknowledges that the owned name was not appropriate without turning the explanation into a defense.
  • Make consequential changes with the teamMike describes soliciting internal feedback rather than treating the rename as a founder-only decree.

Transcript summary

  1. Complex states the controversy directly

    The report opens by explaining that Chinatown Market had been criticized for turning the history and lived reality of Chinatowns into a commercial joke. Diet Prada’s posts helped move that criticism into a wider public discussion.

  2. The conversation asks about accountability

    At the company’s Los Angeles headquarters, Complex asks Mike to explain the original name, the public response, the renaming process, and what accountability should mean inside streetwear.

  3. The name began with Canal Street shirt references

    Mike recalls a friend proposing a familiar profanity shirt sold in tourist shops. That led quickly to Thank You Have a Nice Day bags, smiley faces, bootleg humor, and other references associated with Canal Street retail.

  4. A quick working name became a company

    The phrase Chinatown Market was chosen within minutes, without considering what ownership of that name would imply at scale. Mike initially saw a legal pop-culture mashup system, not the business or controversy it would become.

  5. Mike says Chinatown was not his name to own

    Looking back, he accepts that good intentions do not create a right to a community’s name. MARKET keeps the company’s participatory ethos while removing the claim embedded in the earlier identity.

  6. The team and community remain the intended center

    Mike says the company will continue highlighting staff and building a less exclusive streetwear community. The immediate priority is investing in the people who made the brand possible rather than treating the rename as a cosmetic campaign.

  7. Public watchdogs have a role

    He accepts that social accounts can call out companies and that accountability is useful. His qualification is that criticism should leave room for conversation, learning, and evidence that a person or organization has changed.

  8. Growth does not erase responsibility

    Mike rejects the idea that harmful behavior should be ignored, but also rejects a system where every mistake permanently defines the person. He frames the desired outcome as stopping harm and requiring improvement.

  9. The issue is not whether every accusation was precise

    Asked whether the company was painted with too broad a brush, Mike returns to the underlying name. Even if particular products were not deliberately insulting, the identity created confusion and claimed something that did not belong to the founders.

  10. Historical coercion changes the meaning of the reference

    He acknowledges that Chinatowns were formed by exclusion, immigration pressure, and forced geographic concentration—not simply as aesthetic marketplaces. That history makes an outsider’s casual commercial use fundamentally different from ordinary visual inspiration.

  11. The report shifts to Welcome to Chinatown

    Complex introduces the grassroots New York organization Welcome to Chinatown, which supports local businesses and community voices. The group participated in discussions with MARKET about the rename.

  12. The organization formed during neighborhood decline

    Its founders describe themselves as Chinatown residents responding to business closures, anti-Asian rhetoric, and the fear that pandemic-era racism would accelerate displacement and gentrification.

  13. Anti-Asian narratives are experienced as cyclical

    The founders connect COVID-era language with racism their families experienced during SARS. Their work tries to replace an ignorant outside narrative with the stories, labor, and continuity of neighborhood businesses.

  14. Chinatown is more than a transaction

    Welcome to Chinatown emphasizes immigrant effort, generational values, and the people behind storefronts. Preserving the neighborhood means seeing those relationships instead of treating the district as a consumable theme.

  15. Young New Yorkers already knew the streetwear brand

    Because its volunteer community is young and culturally engaged, the organization was familiar with Chinatown Market. Many volunteers had already questioned why the brand used the name while not being made by or for the neighborhood.

  16. The initial response to the brand was negative

    The name felt insulting rather than flattering to the organization. Mike and Dan’s decision to contact them was unexpected because the community had no reason to assume the company would invite real criticism.

  17. The first conversation was met with skepticism

    Welcome to Chinatown had seen companies perform concern for social media and questioned whether MARKET wanted genuine change. The group agreed to engage because criticism is only useful if it can lead to a different behavior.

  18. Support should follow the community’s mission

    The organization warns brands against vague claims that they are elevating Asian culture. A useful relationship should align with local priorities, personal values, and work the company can sustain beyond one public controversy.

  19. The rename included internal and external voices

    Mike says the process began with the full MARKET team rather than a private founder decision. Community leaders then supplied additional feedback on the name and on what material support could be offered during pandemic travel restrictions.

  20. Hard conversations are part of organizational learning

    Without the option of flying in for workshops, the company focused on communication and other ways to help. Mike says the uncomfortable exchanges mattered because easy affirmation would not reveal what the business needed to change.

  21. Streetwear has a daily social responsibility

    Mike argues that advocacy cannot be limited to occasional statements. The people hired, the voices given a platform, the reasons behind partnerships, and the backgrounds represented in the work all express the brand’s accountability.

  22. Product quality is not the only obligation

    MARKET still has to make good products, but Mike says it must also model respectful behavior for its community. The renaming process was an opportunity to demonstrate listening and considered action rather than defensive explanation.

  23. Listening has to precede self-defense

    Mike’s final contribution is that intention alone cannot settle the issue. A company has to hear affected people before speaking for itself and accept that respect may require changing a valuable identity.

  24. Complex closes with its own editorial position

    The narrator argues that streetwear grew from marginalized and outsider communities, so it inherits a responsibility to build community rather than appropriating one. The report ends by inviting viewers into the broader debate.
