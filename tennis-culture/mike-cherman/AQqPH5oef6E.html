Behind The Hype | Interview with Mike Cherman

Transcript summary

  1. The interview begins with accumulated experience

    Host Alan Neal frames Mike as someone whose streetwear knowledge came through both success and loss. The conversation is intended to give emerging designers a practical account of how a brand is actually built.

  2. Mikey Merchandise learned through selling

    Mike traces the story to shirts sold from his car in high school, punishment for selling on campus, one year at Parsons, and a Lower East Side mentor who gave him real design work inside New York’s emerging streetwear network.

  3. A Nike basement supplied hands-on access

    At Nike Bowery Stadium, Mike made custom pieces for visiting artists and athletes. When the space closed, he bought some of its machines cheaply and converted institutional equipment into the production base for ICNY.

  4. ICNY translated cycling crashes into clothing

    Several collisions while biking and running at night led Mike to build reflective apparel for his own safety. The first brand began with a lived problem and became his first attempt to organize product, identity, and distribution.

  5. Excitement concealed a dangerous investment agreement

    A young founder accepted investor control because any sacrifice seemed justified by the opportunity. Several years later, those terms allowed the investor to remove Mike from the company he had created.

  6. Termination forced identity away from one company

    The dismissal arrived as a blunt business message despite Mike’s emotional and physical identification with ICNY. Starting again required accepting public failure and separating his ability to create from ownership of the old brand.

  7. A Frank Ocean bootleg restarted momentum

    Mike combined a current album reference with a Nike mark and sold the shirt at ComplexCon. Its immediate response showed that fast cultural observation and competent graphic execution could produce demand.

  8. Fifty thousand dollars in orders disappeared

    A quick online launch generated extraordinary sales, then Frank Ocean’s team sent an infringement notice. Mike refunded every order, losing the revenue but gaining proof that rapid internet-speed product development was a real capability.

  9. The failure clarified the business model

    He concluded that the company did not need large production infrastructure at the start. It needed creativity, quick testing, and a legal way to translate memes and pop culture without depending on infringing work.

  10. Provocation can open a door but carries a line

    Mike says some unofficial work is designed to attract the attention of the company being referenced. He describes the strategy as asking forgiveness after a meaningful transformation, while acknowledging that inspiration and theft are not the same thing.

  11. The unofficial Converse shoe became a licensed relationship

    A hybrid shoe worn by prominent athletes circulated widely before Converse contacted MARKET. The attention led to an official event, demonstrating how a visible experiment can become a conversation when the source company sees new demand.

  12. Transformation has to produce a new point of view

    Mike argues that borrowing is defensible only when the maker reshapes a reference into their own language. The skill is not discovering an untouched image; it is making an existing code legible to a different audience.

  13. MARKET is presented as identification, not erasure

    After criticism of the Chinatown Market name, Mike says the shortened name identifies what the company had become: a market of people, internet culture, staff contributions, and public participation.

  14. Young employees help steer the company

    Mike rejects the idea that junior staff should remain silent. He wants a workplace where people can influence direction, see that their perspective matters, and experience a more democratic creative organization.

  15. Broad distribution was an inclusion strategy

    Selling through Urban Outfitters prompted claims that the brand was finished, but the retailer introduced it to stylists, artists, and many consumers who would never enter a rarefied boutique.

  16. Community matters more than artificial scarcity

    MARKET appears in luxury stores and mass retailers because Mike prefers a wide invitation. He argues that a supported community can preserve relevance better than an exclusive account list built on fragile boutique economics.

  17. Making replaced earlier identities

    Mike recalls basketball ambitions, drawing sneakers in class, injury, and not fully fitting into established groups. The high-school shirt that provoked a fight was the first object he made that produced an undeniable reaction.

  18. Reaction turned a hobby into a direction

    Seeing people care about something created with his own hands separated design from earlier jobs. That emotional proof gave Mike a reason to focus his time and tolerate the uncertainty of pursuing fashion.

  19. New York access was more important than the diploma

    Parsons gave Mike one year in the city, but his education came from freelancing and working after class. While peers socialized, he spent nights accumulating the hours and relationships needed to function as a designer.

  20. Self-teaching produced a makeshift aesthetic

    Online tools and experimentation led Mike toward stretched type, imperfect construction, and fast visual decisions. What formal design culture once dismissed as amateur later became part of mainstream graphic language.

  21. Imperfection became a recognizable voice

    Trying to meet perfect design standards eventually gave way to embracing unusual, wonky decisions. Mike treats the tension between polish and immediacy as a source of authenticity rather than a flaw to remove.

  22. Using a tool visibly can matter more than using it first

    Critics accused Mike of taking the handheld print-gun idea from earlier artists. His response is that repeated events, installations, and widely shared demonstrations turned the tool into a meaningful part of MARKET’s language.

  23. Fear of failure is usually larger than its audience

    Mike tells emerging designers that other people quickly return to their own problems. A weak release, low engagement, or criticism feels permanent to the maker, but rarely receives that level of sustained attention from everyone else.

  24. Commercial fashion still needs a reason beyond transaction

    He acknowledges that selling shirts is business, but rejects a life organized only around deals. Teaching and returning knowledge to the young people who support the brand gives the commercial work a broader purpose.

  25. Learn every small job before trying to direct the whole

    The closing advice is to intern, sew, sell, produce, and place yourself in as many useful situations as possible. Mike does not recommend permanent apprenticeship; he recommends learning enough of the system to operate independently.

  26. The host closes on access and accumulated knowledge

    Alan summarizes MARKET as a platform for people and thanks Mike for sharing the practical knowledge behind the public brand story.
