Mike Cherman: How Bootlegging, Culture, and Creativity Built Chinatown Market

podcast2025-10-1302:02:46

Summary

  • A two-hour retrospective on learning the streetwear business from the floor up, building through bootlegs and fast cultural response, then turning those instincts into teams, collaborations, stores, and MARKET.
  • Goodwood made the industry tangibleMike’s first New York role involved assembling wooden jewelry during the blog era. Starting with repetitive shop work put him near the products, artists, and information networks he wanted to understand.

Transcript summary

  1. The two-hour conversation opens with a less public part of Mike's life

    The hosts introduce a broad career retrospective that also discusses cannabis, stress, creativity, and culture. Mike treats personal habits and business decisions as connected rather than presenting a sanitized founder profile.

  2. Goodwood made the streetwear economy tangible

    His first New York role involved repetitive assembly of wooden jewelry and custom objects. Working on the floor placed him near artists, buyers, managers, equipment, and the information networks of the blog era.

  3. Early design fees were guesses made without business context

    Mike remembers proposing prices without understanding a client's budget, rights, or future value. Looking back, he would begin with questions about scope and economics rather than allowing eagerness to define the deal.

  4. Business was learned by standing near transactions

    He watched experienced operators negotiate, collect payment, plan collections, and follow through while doing ordinary production labor. Observation became valuable because he could connect it to real tasks and consequences.

  5. Employee departures require self-examination

    Mike describes a former colleague whose exit once felt personal, then recognizes his own management mistakes and the employee's need to pursue a different path. Leadership maturity means learning without casting every separation as betrayal.

  6. Customization work connected machinery to celebrity storytelling

    A room full of cutters, presses, printers, and blanks let Mike become the patch and graphics person. Custom requests taught him how one person's references can be translated into a jacket or object with emotional specificity.

  7. Scenes move faster than their founding mythology

    Mike compares early ComplexCon crowds in graphic tees with later aesthetics shaped by different music, silhouettes, and online performance. A brand cannot demand that culture remain frozen at the moment it first understood it.

  8. Cannabis functioned as both reward and retreat

    He explains that smoking could help him decompress after concentrated work while also making him withdraw from friends and conversation. The effect depended on context, dose, and whether the habit supported or avoided the life around it.

  9. Public mistakes do not erase the full body of work

    The hosts discuss the pain of friends reducing someone to one disliked action. Mike tries to accept criticism, correct genuine harm, and still remember that a career contains hundreds of other decisions that one moment cannot fully summarize.

  10. Creative criticism from friends carries unusual weight

    Feedback is hardest when it comes from people who know the entire journey. Mike distinguishes a useful challenge from a relationship in which judgment becomes identity, and he tries not to build future work only from defensiveness.

  11. California became a system of product details

    For a shoe project, Mike used drive-through line graphics, palm trees, a burger detail, pool color, and checkerboard references. The collaboration became specific through accumulated observation rather than a generic state name.

  12. Collaboration can trigger conflicts between adjacent partners

    A checker pattern drew concern from another footwear company preparing its own project with MARKET. Mike describes learning that each partner reads shared cultural symbols through contracts, category ownership, and competitive timing.

  13. The early brand ran like a public atelier

    A morning idea could be designed, photographed, posted, made, and shipped by the next day. Repeated Chinatown graphics and technique variations generated traction because the audience watched the workshop learn in real time.

  14. Cannabis and streetwear share collaboration potential and scarcity drama

    The hosts compare industries built on identity, limited drops, and informal networks. Mike sees enormous room for product and storytelling, but warns that status conflicts and guarded access can prevent mature partnerships.

  15. Strong cannabis brands build a world around the product

    Packaging, environments, characters, and retail experiences can make each strain or maker legible. The comparison to Pokémon and long-running lifestyle brands shows how narrative systems help a commodity carry distinct meaning.

  16. Premium packaging can become wasteful theater

    As a product designer, Mike appreciates a layered unboxing experience but questions plastic, nested boxes, and cost that do not improve the cannabis. Good design has to account for disposal and use, not only the first reveal.

  17. An unofficial Grateful Dead idea became licensed study

    Rather than ending with a takedown, the initial bootleg opened a conversation about working officially. Mike then spent time with the music and community so the collaboration would represent more than borrowed iconography.

  18. Online display changed how street culture forms

    He contrasts Fairfax lines, block parties, and repeated physical encounters with scenes now optimized for a post. The concern is not simple nostalgia: distribution technology changes how trust develops and how a community proves it exists.

  19. A high standard should begin with one's own work

    Mike questions the usefulness of obsessing over every other company's flaws. A maker can appreciate someone's best output without endorsing everything, while still holding personal decisions to a demanding and consistent standard.

  20. Cannabis has not settled on one cultural register

    Luxury hospitality, street retail, medical use, and ordinary consumption require different design languages. Mike expects multiple brands to coexist rather than one elevated aesthetic becoming the category's final form.

  21. Bootlegging has a boundary even for a bootlegger

    Mike distinguishes interpretive borrowing from a company reproducing his finished design and selling it as its own. The line is imperfect, but transformation, commentary, scale, and direct substitution all affect how he judges the act.

  22. Useful merchandise starts with the audience's behavior

    He praises the hosts for making rolling and ash objects rather than defaulting to logo shirts. Product strategy improves when it solves a ritual already present in the community instead of treating merchandise as automatic.

  23. A brand can operate as a production bridge

    MARKET can find a specialist ceramic maker, develop an object, bring another company's identity, and connect the parties. That agency role creates value through sourcing, translation, campaign building, and trust—not just a logo application.

  24. Opinion is valuable only from selected truth-tellers

    Mike limits major feedback to people with relevant knowledge who will challenge him. Friends who praise everything protect comfort but cannot reveal a weak product, while random opinions may lack enough context to guide the decision.

  25. Former employees extend the company's impact

    People who learned inside Mike's organizations later built their own brands and careers, sometimes after difficult endings. He regards that transfer of capability as part of the result even when personal relationships did not remain clean.

  26. Designing for a younger self requires present-day listening

    Mike often asks whether an object would have thrilled him at eighteen or twenty, but recognizes that memory alone cannot represent current consumers. Teams and communities help update the instinct without discarding the emotional source.

  27. Growth makes repetition harder and identity more important

    After many collaborations, licenses, and bootlegs, obvious combinations have been exhausted. MARKET has to return to its underlying humor, accessibility, and making culture rather than using past breadth as a substitute for a new idea.

  28. Conflict does not cancel pride in people's growth

    Mike recounts painful employee histories and acknowledges behavior that damaged trust. He still takes satisfaction in seeing people apply skills learned around the company, holding harm and contribution in the same record.

  29. The closing advice is to begin before permission

    Mike had limited resources, made side income, showed products to the people already doing the work, and kept entering the conversation. He urges makers to tolerate judgment and ask respected people concrete questions because useful outreach often receives an answer.

  30. The retrospective ends with persistence rather than a shortcut

    The hosts close on the distance between a finished public brand and the repeated low-status work beneath it. Mike's through-line is making, showing up, revising the system, and continuing after both attention and failure.
