Full interview, in order

  1. 00:00:00

    A long career interview begins before the recognizable brands

    Mike traces high-school shirts, sports, school, early jobs, ICNY, partnerships, and Chinatown Market. The length allows him to distinguish the public milestones from the operating problems that connected them.

  2. 00:04:46

    Personal savings became the first inventory

    As a teenager he used gifted money to buy blank shirts and committed to a quantity before understanding demand. The decision forced him to learn printing, selling, and the emotional cost of product that does not move.

  3. 00:09:59

    The end of a sports identity made room for making

    Basketball had structured Mike's ambition and social life, so losing that path left a genuine vacancy. Clothing and graphics became the next discipline he could practice repeatedly rather than a casual backup interest.

  4. 00:14:46

    Formal school was not the expected curriculum

    Mike entered a new Parsons program believing it combined design and business, then found a different emphasis. The mismatch pushed much of the useful education into internships, jobs, freelance deadlines, and self-directed software work.

  5. 00:18:40

    Entry work provided access to equipment and decisions

    Customization shops and small brands let Mike handle files, patches, production machinery, and client requests. The tasks were basic, but proximity revealed how cultural ideas become priced and deliverable products.

  6. 00:24:51

    Fast usefulness opened an unexpected Kith relationship

    Quick graphic and production help in a customization environment placed Mike beside Ronnie Fieg during early work. Solving the immediate problem mattered more than arriving through a formal creative-director introduction.

  7. 00:29:20

    ICNY turned reflectivity into a focused proposition

    The first major brand connected cycling visibility with streetwear aesthetics. A clear material idea helped it earn attention, while the narrow premise also created pressure to keep every collection inside one technical story.

  8. 00:34:48

    Wholesale scale amplified a product mismatch

    An appealing outdoor retailer created the appearance of major progress, but ICNY's assortment did not fully satisfy that technical customer. Distribution made unresolved design and positioning problems more expensive rather than correcting them.

  9. 00:39:40

    Partnership structure lagged behind creative momentum

    Mike describes agreements and advisers that were not understood with enough precision while the company grew. Excitement about expansion allowed ownership, authority, and accountability questions to remain dangerously vague.

  10. 00:44:47

    The collapse had shared causes

    He accepts his inexperience and mistakes while also describing partners who shifted blame. The useful reading avoids both total innocence and total self-condemnation: multiple weak systems interacted until the brand could not continue.

  11. 00:50:00

    After loss, service work restored financial footing

    Graphics, printing, consulting, and production projects provided income when Mike no longer controlled ICNY. Those jobs proved he could remain useful, but they did not replace his desire to build a product platform he owned.

  12. 00:54:49

    A friend challenged him to stop waiting for a perfect return

    Mike kept talking about making something as important as the lost brand. The blunt response was to make anything, and a shirt request arriving days later supplied the concrete action that became Chinatown Market.

  13. 00:57:30

    The second company began with fewer illusions

    Small drops, direct sales, and rapid feedback reduced the need for a large seasonal bet. Mike reused the production skills and contacts from the earlier company while changing how inventory and cultural timing were handled.

  14. 01:00:02

    Streetwear purity can disguise weak business logic

    Mike questions why broad demand is celebrated in most industries but condemned as selling out in streetwear. Reach is not automatically wise, yet scarcity should not substitute for examining margins, service, and the audience the company actually wants.

  15. 01:05:13

    Refunding a fast hit clarified the opportunity

    A topical release crossed a trademark boundary, forcing Mike to return every order manually. The loss still demonstrated that the team could notice, design, sell, and fulfill rapidly if the system were applied to legitimate material.

  16. 01:08:20

    Bootleg debate exposes inconsistent standards

    Mike notes that small brands are publicly criticized while mass retailers reproduce cultural ideas at much greater scale. He does not claim copying is harmless; he asks for clearer distinctions between commentary, transformation, and direct commercial duplication.

  17. 01:12:20

    Trusted production partners are invisible infrastructure

    He credits a long-term collaborator with making multiple projects possible through sourcing, quality control, and honest problem-solving. Creative output depends on these durable operational relationships even when the public sees only the founder.

  18. 01:15:12

    Contracts now precede committed labor

    The ICNY experience changed Mike's threshold for beginning a partnership. He no longer lets excitement justify months of work before signed terms, clear roles, and genuine comfort with the people controlling the project.

  19. 01:17:40

    New ventures are separated by audience and function

    Mike describes simultaneous projects with different price, technical, retail, and geographic purposes. The structure is meant to keep one brand from carrying every idea while still sharing learned capabilities behind the scenes.

  20. 01:19:30

    The retrospective ends with disciplined optimism

    The career still depends on acting before certainty, but the mature version adds contracts, demand tests, defined partners, and product-market fit. Failure remains part of the work; it should not remain unprocessed repetition.
