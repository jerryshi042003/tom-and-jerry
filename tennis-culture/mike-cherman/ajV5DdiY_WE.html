Full interview, in order

  1. 00:00:15

    Telli introduces a conversation about life and growth

    The host welcomes Mike, identifies him as MARKET’s founder, and frames the episode as a discussion of success, relationships, and the life surrounding a growing creative company.

  2. 00:00:45

    Mike entered streetwear from the back end

    He describes an insider industry where relationships matter, then traces his route through high-school shirts sold from his car rather than through an established fashion position.

  3. 00:01:14

    A fight turned school merchandise into a story

    A humorous shirt featuring another student sold out before the subject received one. The resulting fight brought Mike, his parents, and the school administration into a conflict over selling on campus.

  4. 00:01:43

    The school reaction provided first validation

    The shirt was the first thing Mike made that produced an intense response. Unlike basketball, where he was not the strongest player, design felt like an activity in which sustained effort might make him exceptional.

  5. 00:02:12

    Parsons led to a street-level apprenticeship

    After entering Parsons, Mike spent time with Chase Infinite, then connected to A$AP Rocky’s circle. At first he was simply an intern doing ordinary work inside a store where important people passed through.

  6. 00:02:40

    Dropping out was one step, not a magic breakthrough

    A year of school and after-class work gave Mike enough confidence to leave Parsons. The mentor connection did not make him successful immediately, but it led to a Nike customization job with equipment and constant making.

  7. 00:03:09

    Creative access met a personal cycling problem

    The Nike space let Mike produce many custom shirts, but the key product idea arrived after he was hit while biking to work. He used the available tools to test reflective socks the following day.

  8. 00:03:37

    ICNY scaled and was lost through a bad ownership deal

    Reflective running and cycling apparel became a multi-year brand. Mike accepted a million-dollar investment and gave away controlling ownership, eventually receiving notice that he had been removed from his own company.

  9. 00:04:06

    Watching friends progress triggered the restart

    Back in California, broke and living in a friend’s room, Mike accompanied the Pleasures team to work. When they prepared for international pop-ups, he confronted the fact that he was watching instead of building.

  10. 00:04:35

    Five graphics formed the first Chinatown Market set

    A friend’s Canal Street shirt idea expanded into smiley faces, Thank You bag references, and a Frank Ocean/Nike graphic. The small set gave a loose downtown-New-York idea enough form to test at ComplexCon.

  11. 00:05:04

    A sold-out booth led to a rapid online launch

    The Frank Ocean shirt sold through its tiny stock in an hour. Mike created a dedicated site, watched Shopify register roughly fifty thousand dollars in orders, and briefly believed the new brand had solved his financial problems.

  12. 00:05:33

    Refunding the hit revealed the legal constraint

    Frank Ocean’s team sent an infringement notice and Mike returned the revenue. The experience still identified his real advantage: conceive in the morning, publish by noon, and fulfill the following day—provided the idea could survive legally.

  13. 00:06:02

    MARKET grew from handwritten labels to a team

    The company expanded from Mike making individual shirts and neck labels in a small room to roughly twenty-five employees near Pasadena. He wants the organization itself to model a more open next generation of brand building.

  14. 00:06:30

    The original name required a public reckoning

    Mike says the Canal Street inspiration did not account for the historical and cultural claim made by Chinatown Market. During the pandemic, the company chose to change rather than defend a name that had outgrown its casual origin.

  15. 00:06:59

    Scale creates obligations the side project did not anticipate

    A phrase that seemed interpretable inside one New York reference became a global business identity. Mike accepts that Chinatowns reflect constrained settlement and exclusion, so the company could have chosen another name.

  16. 00:07:28

    MARKET describes the operating system more accurately

    The shortened name matches a company that follows internet time, responds to pop culture, and gives familiar nostalgia a new form. The change keeps the work method while dropping the ownership claim.

  17. 00:07:57

    Celebrity work is used to reactivate a public story

    Mike cites Erykah Badu and Mike Tyson projects where MARKET helped frame a new merchandise or cultural moment. The objective is not celebrity proximity alone, but supplying a perspective that makes an existing figure feel newly discoverable.

  18. 00:08:26

    The best collaboration sits between memory and discovery

    MARKET looks for references that adults roughly Mike’s age recognize emotionally while younger customers can approach as something new. Too much nostalgia becomes repetition; too little history loses the intergenerational connection.

  19. 00:08:54

    Collectible objects combine several long-term interests

    The smiley basketball joins Mike’s love of sport with his preference for approachable graphic objects. Collectibility emerges because the object carries a recognizable feeling, not because the company simply limits quantity.

  20. 00:09:22

    The sports message is participation rather than elite promise

    Mike restates the line as ‘you can do it too’ instead of ‘just do it.’ Most people will never become professional athletes, but a smiling ball can memorialize being a fan and enjoying the game.

  21. 00:09:50

    An icon crosses categories when the emotion survives

    The smiley can move from basketballs to boxing gloves because it adds lightness to serious equipment. The ordinary object remains usable while gaining a personality that can live beyond one sports category.

  22. 00:10:18

    Fun is part of how a child reaches commitment

    Mike and the host discuss happiness, youth sports, and the need to let children enjoy a practice before demanding professional seriousness. Competition and play do not have to cancel one another.

  23. 00:10:46

    Effort matters without pretending every result is equal

    They reject both joyless pressure and empty participation awards. A person can compete, fail, learn, and still value the experience of trying a craft in which someone else is more advanced.

  24. 00:11:16

    Different people can lead in different domains

    The host imagines a game show where neither of them would win at basketball but each might excel in fashion, speaking, or modeling. The larger point is to recognize the field in which one’s effort becomes meaningful.

  25. 00:11:45

    Consistency often beats unused talent

    Mike argues that a moderately skilled person who repeatedly appears can pass a more gifted person who does not. He does not call himself the best illustrator or designer; he calls himself willing to risk embarrassment and keep entering the room.

  26. 00:12:13

    Believing he belongs supports continued exposure

    Mike’s confidence is not a claim that every product is superior. It is the operating belief that he deserves to participate and can keep improving if he places his work in public.

  27. 00:12:41

    Work has consumed too much of the available life

    Asked about family and friends, Mike admits that balancing them remains difficult. Years of arriving early, leaving late, and defining himself through work made disconnection unfamiliar.

  28. 00:13:10

    Reinvestment became both strategy and compulsion

    Mike describes skipping lunch, avoiding nightlife, taking little money personally, and directing resources back into the team. The choice helped the company, but also made it easy to neglect himself and other relationships.

  29. 00:13:39

    A partner cannot receive only leftover attention

    He recognizes that a company receiving obsessive care creates disparity in a romantic relationship. The same attention used to nurture work has to be deliberately applied to the people around him.

  30. 00:14:07

    Counting remaining visits changes family priorities

    A comedy routine about seeing one’s parents only a handful of times per year made Mike calculate how few meetings may remain. That perspective challenges his habit of shortening calls or leaving family time for work.

  31. 00:14:35

    Providing for family does not settle the time conflict

    Business can create the freedom and resources to care for loved ones, but it also consumes the hours one hoped to share with them. Mike treats this tension as unresolved rather than offering a clean productivity rule.

  32. 00:15:04

    Obsession moves into tennis and collecting

    The same intensity appears when Mike starts tennis: he watches instruction, takes lessons, and tries to improve quickly. Rug collecting similarly expands into a large archive, showing that deep consumption and learning are general traits, not only work habits.

  33. 00:15:33

    The episode shifts into a hypothetical game

    The host and Mike begin several rounds of ‘would you rather.’ The first choice contrasts eating only food paste forever with entering a bullfight once without training.

  34. 00:16:02

    One severe risk seems preferable to permanent sensory loss

    Both favor attempting the bullfight because they value food texture and variety. The joking exchange is less strategic than the interview, but reveals Mike’s tendency to choose a bounded ordeal over lifelong restriction.

  35. 00:16:30

    A clone is preferable to visible permanent discomfort

    Given a choice between a perfect replacement trying to take over his life and unremovable nostril hair, Mike chooses the clone and jokes that a duplicate could handle work while he rests.

  36. 00:17:58

    Temporary injury beats permanent restriction

    Mike would rather drop a heavy barbell on his foot once than wear a neck brace for life. He also chooses permanently open eyes over having no mouth because speaking and eating matter too much.

  37. 00:19:26

    The tennis-ball-head question becomes a motorcycle risk

    A permanent head the size of a tennis ball feels so socially unlivable that Mike says he would attempt a fifty-foot motorcycle jump despite expecting serious injury. The host relates her own difficulty handling a large motorcycle.

  38. 00:20:23

    Whispering is easier than compulsory intense eye contact

    For the final hypothetical, Mike chooses a permanently quiet voice over having to stare intensely at every person he addresses. The game ends and the conversation returns to MARKET’s current work.

  39. 00:21:21

    MARKET promises unpredictable releases

    Mike directs listeners to the brand’s channels and describes the public feed as a stream of unusual products, visitors, and happenings rather than a conventional seasonal catalog.

  40. 00:21:50

    Visitors can become temporary characters inside the office

    He recalls Hasbulla arriving around exotic cars, Mike Tyson taking over creative and shipping roles, and a broad Grateful Dead launch amplified by LeBron wearing the collection.

  41. 00:22:19

    Range is part of the creative pleasure

    Collaborations can move from Dennis Rodman to My Chemical Romance because Mike enjoys jumping between communities. The challenge is to connect those worlds without reducing each to the same MARKET graphic.

  42. 00:22:47

    New participants do not need to pass a purity test

    When Grateful Dead fans demanded that LeBron prove his knowledge, Mike rejected the gatekeeping. A collaboration can introduce unfamiliar people to a culture and let them appreciate it at different depths.

  43. 00:23:15

    Every product is another attempt to open a world

    Mike connects the host’s podcast and his own objects through the same aim: create an inviting point of entry. Success does not end the problem; each release renews the challenge of making someone curious or happy.

  44. 00:23:45

    The closing brand position holds access and distinction together

    Mike lists public channels and broad retailers, then describes MARKET as inclusive while still creating unusual, desirable objects. The stated mission is to show young people why they can participate too.
