Iconic Podcast Full Interview with Mike Cherman of Chinatown Market

podcast2019-04-1500:17:38

Summary

  • A short podcast interview on the Mikey Merchandise origin, what ‘making it’ means, building a company that supports other creative people, and Mike’s preference for making brands over protecting a title.
  • The first audience was classmatesSelling shirts and conflict with school administration revealed that the activity could become more than a hobby.
  • Success is not a finished stateMike distinguishes career milestones from being satisfied or done.
  • The company supports other makersHe values sustaining a team and giving creative people a platform, not only producing his own graphics.
  • Titles matter less than the repeated behaviorHe returns to designing products and starting brands as an ongoing way of living.

Transcript summary

  1. The host introduces a former neighbor

    The Iconic Podcast presents Mike as Chinatown Market’s founder and explains that the interview has a personal connection: the host knew Mike when they lived near one another in California.

  2. The public profile begins with a sharp life pivot

    The host reads Mike’s account of breaking his hand while playing basketball, then shifting toward cannabis and T-shirt making. Selling at school created the Mikey Merchandise nickname.

  3. Campus distribution made the activity feel real

    Administrative conflict over selling shirts was the first institutional reaction to Mike’s work. The host treats the nickname and story as evidence that the later brand did not appear without a long period of making.

  4. Early promotion was physical and local

    The host remembers Mike’s earlier Newcastle brand and a stack of stickers distributed around their neighborhood. Before celebrity placement or major retail, building an audience meant getting friends to place the mark on skateboards, basketball hoops, and street fixtures.

  5. Commitment developed through continued discovery

    Mike resists naming one age at which he fully committed. Like the host’s podcast, the career grew from repeatedly exploring an interest until the accumulation of work began to resemble a profession.

  6. A passion can become a career without a degree

    Mike notes that he did not finish college, but his thirties find him operating the kind of creative company that formal credentials were supposed to enable. The path was continuous practice rather than one conversion moment.

  7. ‘Making it’ depends on the measure

    Asked whether he has made it, Mike says he has achieved recognizable company milestones but remains far from satisfied. Public success and internal completion are different questions.

  8. The company still feels early from inside

    Mike has a longer list of things he wants to build than the outside image suggests. Celebrity visibility can make the brand look finished, while the founder experiences it as an organization still learning its range.

  9. Famous wearers provide real validation

    The host lists LeBron James, Kendrick Lamar, Lil Wayne, Pharrell, and DJ Khaled. Mike accepts that by ordinary measures the company is doing well, even if acknowledging that does not reduce his urgency.

  10. A sinking-ship mentality preserves drive

    Mike says he behaves as though some part of the operation always needs saving. The metaphor keeps him attentive, but he also allows that the present work is fun and materially successful.

  11. The platform supports more makers than the founder

    The daily privilege is not only making shirts. Chinatown Market sustains employees who can exercise their own creativity through a shared product and distribution system.

  12. Bootlegging is misunderstood as the entire business

    Mike accepts that the brand makes unofficial riffs, but rejects the idea that it is built only on stealing. The unauthorized object is a quick experiment or burst of cultural energy, not the complete long-term operating model.

  13. An unofficial object can show the thinking

    He describes bootlegs as a way to execute an impossible idea rapidly and demonstrate a point of view. The ethical line is whether the company is exploiting the source as a permanent substitute for original business.

  14. The handheld printer became a public signature

    The host calls the device Mike’s logo gun and asks whether anyone uses it better. Mike says the technology existed already; MARKET repurposed and demonstrated it at enough scale to help popularize the tool.

  15. Viral use does not equal invention

    Mike enjoys watching other people adopt the printer but does not claim to have created it. The company’s contribution was a visible application and the chain of new practices that followed.

  16. Advice to twenty-one: remain hungry and ask for help

    Mike would preserve his younger ambition while removing the performance of knowing everything. Asking for expertise and surrounding a weak area with capable people is different from faking certainty.

  17. A creative founder still has to learn business

    His main industry frustration is watching people profit from a look without operating responsibly. If someone wants to own a company, they should understand the commercial work instead of immediately outsourcing every function.

  18. Outsourcing before understanding creates dependency

    Service companies can help a brand grow, but Mike believes founders who never learn fulfillment, finance, production, or distribution cannot judge whether those services are building the right company.

  19. The title matters less than the repeated activity

    With several projects in progress, entrepreneur is an available label, but Mike identifies more strongly with designing products and starting brands. The work is a lifestyle before it is a formal title.

  20. The conversation moves into personal details

    Mike describes his first tattoo as an ouroboros—a reminder that choices return—and a later tired Mickey image that reflects the fatigue of continued work.

  21. Ping-pong lessons become team building

    The host asks about MARKET staff receiving instruction from a former professional player. Mike describes it as a fun shared activity and jokes that he remains one of the stronger office competitors.

  22. Food and childhood memories lighten the interview

    They compare In-N-Out orders and then discuss early Call of Duty sessions with Mike’s brother. Mike no longer makes much time for games because work occupies most of his attention.

  23. Low-effort leisure is used for disconnection

    When Mike does relax, he prefers something that does not require much mental activation. Basketball at the warehouse supplies another kind of break, including competitive games and improbable elimination shots.

  24. International support broadens the reference frame

    Mike identifies Paris, Hong Kong, Japan, and other overseas markets as receptive to the brand. More important than sales, travel teaches that American habits and assumptions are not the complete world.

  25. Unexpected collaborations remain the attraction

    The host points to the Crocs project, and Mike says the appeal lies in making something people did not predict. Surprise is a product goal, not only a promotional tactic.

  26. The host closes with a personal success witness

    After Mike leaves, the host recalls their neighborhood connection and emphasizes how striking it is to see the former local shirt maker become a globally visible streetwear operator while still speaking in unfinished terms.
