Full interview, in order

  1. 00:00:00

    Penthouse Sessions arrives in Seoul

    The host introduces a panel format previously used in Hong Kong and Shanghai. Guests represent Mischief, Chinatown Market, and IISE inside Rise Hotel with Seoul Community Radio.

  2. 00:00:36

    Each guest names a different operating role

    Soojin handles Mischief’s international marketing, public relations, and sales; Mike describes Chinatown Market plus its Los Angeles creative and production agency; Kevin identifies himself as IISE’s co-founder and community designer.

  3. 00:01:09

    The setting is Seoul Fashion Week nightlife

    The panel begins after several days around Seoul, with the visitors spending substantial time in Itaewon clubs rather than moving only through formal runway programming.

  4. 00:01:42

    Mischief operates outside the runway calendar

    Soojin says runway shows do not naturally fit Mischief, so the brand stages its own release events. Seoul Fashion Week is not the organization’s primary frame.

  5. 00:02:10

    Mike sees Seoul’s retail and design progress

    Chinatown Market also sits outside runway fashion, but Mike is impressed by the city’s style, stores, and Korean brands gaining international attention. Travel makes the global field of independent labels more visible.

  6. 00:02:40

    IISE entered runway through compressed preparation

    Kevin describes the brand’s first New York show, announced only about six weeks before presentation. A collection not built for runway had to be revised rapidly for the new format.

  7. 00:03:14

    Government support changed what the collection required

    Concept Korea funded IISE and another brand, making the runway possible. The opportunity brought resources but also demanded additional pieces and an unfamiliar presentation language.

  8. 00:03:43

    Mike prefers experiences to runways

    Asked whether Chinatown Market might stage a show, Mike says the more important format is an event where visitors can touch the brand, participate, and understand more than a sequence of looks.

  9. 00:04:13

    A purchase alone gives too little back

    Mike argues that brands owe supporters an experience and access to how things are made. Community grows when customers can see and affect the process rather than only transfer money.

  10. 00:04:41

    Touring workshops turn audiences into makers

    The proposed model is to bring tools to cities such as Seoul and Bangkok, let visitors customize garments, and position them as designers. Inclusiveness replaces the old prestige of keeping most people outside.

  11. 00:05:09

    Office staff are taught to make by hand

    Mike wants retail employees and junior workers to progress toward creating their own products. The company’s role is to demonstrate each step and make independent design seem attainable.

  12. 00:05:37

    Computer design without production knowledge is incomplete

    Every Chinatown Market employee should understand physical construction. Mike criticizes effortless digital output when the designer has no relationship to how the object is printed, sewn, or assembled.

  13. 00:06:05

    Rise Hotel collaborations show different entry points

    Mischief’s hotel shirt came through stockist Works Out and the opening event. The local relationship let a small object introduce new visitors to the brand’s studio.

  14. 00:06:33

    IISE designed both guest and staff experiences

    IISE’s hotel project included room bathrobes and uniforms for front-desk and concierge workers. The collaboration affected how the property operated, not only what was sold in its shop.

  15. 00:07:02

    Mischief begins collaboration with local community

    Soojin explains that Korean musicians, crews, and creative partners are the brand’s first network. International projects are expanding, but the goal remains showing Seoul talent to a wider audience.

  16. 00:08:03

    IISE uses activations for material participation

    Upcoming work includes Gore-Tex, Under Armour, local artists, and a longstanding Korean beer venue. Custom dyeing and live participation connect partner history to the event rather than adding two logos.

  17. 00:08:32

    Mike deliberately chooses some uncool partners

    Original Penguin is useful precisely because the audience does not treat it as the obvious prestigious collaboration. Mike enjoys exploring whether a neglected company can be made newly interesting.

  18. 00:09:02

    No sports company is allowed to own the whole brand

    Mike lists work across Puma, Converse, Nike, and Adidas. Each receives one expression, while Chinatown Market’s durable value remains the employees and community no corporate partner can purchase outright.

  19. 00:09:31

    Collaboration is expected to push limits

    Partners are drawn to the smiley, workshops, inclusiveness, and provocative speed. Mike sees bootlegs as attention experiments that support a larger body of legal work rather than as the final collaboration model.

  20. 00:10:03

    Unofficial smiley work led to a global license

    Urban Outfitters removed an unlicensed smiley shirt after a complaint. The rights holder then approached Chinatown Market, and the relationship expanded into global licensing and larger properties such as SpongeBob.

  21. 00:11:01

    Internet time replaces fashion-calendar delay

    Mike wants an idea developed in the morning, online by noon, and shipped the next day. Designs sitting in line sheets for six months are vulnerable to cultural irrelevance and imitation before release.

  22. 00:11:31

    Speed is treated as a competitive capability

    Other brands sometimes tell Mike that MARKET has released an idea still scheduled far in their future. The lesson is not to hoard concepts until a season makes them formally convenient.

  23. 00:11:59

    Ideas are presented individually rather than as collections

    A continuous sequence of small releases lets the company see which ideas fail and learn immediately. A large seasonal assortment can hide the meaning of each product and delay useful response.

  24. 00:12:27

    Cash flow makes the traditional calendar risky

    Large up-front collections require substantial production money and return it slowly. Releasing continuously allows cheaper exploration and a closer feedback loop with the audience.

  25. 00:12:57

    Each named partner receives a separate investigation

    Timex, Guess, SpongeBob, Converse, and Puma are not interchangeable logo swaps. Mike says the creative value lies in understanding what equity each company holds and adding a relevant MARKET interpretation.

  26. 00:13:53

    Collaboration abundance has reduced automatic excitement

    The panel compares current trade shows with an era when one Nike partnership felt rare. Now an event may effectively require multiple cross-brand products, so the mere fact of collaboration has little meaning.

  27. 00:14:22

    The product and story must replace logo prestige

    Mike says excitement now depends on the actual object, experience, and narrative. Two respected names coming together cannot compensate for an idea with no soul.

  28. 00:14:51

    Offline participation restores some of the lost value

    The other panelists agree that parties, workshops, and physical product encounters matter because simply placing an item online is no longer enough to create memory.

  29. 00:15:20

    The designer is not the sole audience

    Mike says discovery came from realizing he does not design only for himself. Assuming he already knows what everyone wants prevents the consumer from influencing the experience.

  30. 00:15:48

    Dream collaborations can begin outside fashion

    One guest proposes Cadbury, and the discussion turns to mass-market companies. Mike is interested in large partners because they can make a creative idea feel attainable to people outside specialist stores.

  31. 00:16:16

    Mike wants to test ideas in Walmart-scale categories

    He would like to design for an ordinary parent shopping at Walmart or TJ Maxx, even if the product is not branded Chinatown Market. Serving someone unlike himself is a more difficult design problem than proving insider taste.

  32. 00:16:44

    His father demonstrates cross-generational reach

    Mike’s father wears the shirts and is sometimes stopped by young consumers who try to explain the brand to him. The misunderstanding amuses Mike because the object has traveled beyond its founder’s immediate peer group.

  33. 00:17:12

    IISE plans to master clothing before furniture

    The Korean brand began with bags, then moved into apparel and hopes to reach homeware. Kevin argues that category expansion should wait until the team understands its current production.

  34. 00:18:10

    Collaborations can teach a foreign category

    A White Castle project in Shanghai becomes an example of one brand introducing another to an unfamiliar market. Cross-border work is educational when the local partner explains how its scene actually functions.

  35. 00:18:39

    An AOMG idea would connect two communities

    Mike discusses a possible project with DJ Pumpkin and AOMG. He values Korea’s dense support networks and sees a collaboration as a bridge between that local music community and Chinatown Market’s California audience.

  36. 00:19:09

    Tight creative scenes reward and punish behavior

    In Seoul and other Asian cities, Mike observes small but powerful networks where members actively support each other and where mistreating one person can remove access to the wider community.

  37. 00:19:40

    Visible creative careers expand what younger people imagine

    Mike contrasts his own freedom to experiment with friends whose families demanded conventional professions. Workshops, DJs, musicians, and brands can prove that alternative work exists even when failure remains likely.

  38. 00:20:37

    K-pop demonstrates cultural exchange moving both ways

    A Brooklyn-raised Spanish girlfriend can love Korean pop, while the music’s scale reaches Japan, China, and the United States. Mike sees this as evidence that Western culture is no longer the only export.

  39. 00:21:06

    Korean brands challenge better-supported markets

    Strong design can emerge without the retail systems available in the United States. International exposure gives those brands resources and forces American creatives to respond to better execution.

  40. 00:21:37

    Mischief keeps its primary image locally grounded

    International photographers may contribute editorials, but main lookbooks and events focus on Korean women and local creatives. The audience can see people from its own scene wearing and shaping the brand.

  41. 00:22:09

    The brands close with expansion and consistency

    Mischief plans more international collaborations and pop-ups while strengthening its own design quality. IISE prepares runway shows in New York and Seoul.

  42. 00:22:37

    Mike’s final criterion is whether the work remains fun

    ComplexCon schedules and company growth matter less to him than enjoying the people, community, and act of making. If those disappear, the scale is not worth preserving.

  43. 00:23:05

    Making one shirt remains the desired center

    Mike says he works more than he parties or drinks. Even if the business grows into many categories, he wants to retain the direct pleasure of making a shirt every day.

  44. 00:23:33

    The session opens briefly for audience questions

    After the guests outline upcoming shows and projects, the host invites questions and then closes the panel with thanks to the speakers, radio station, and hotel.
