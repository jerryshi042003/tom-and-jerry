Full interview, in order

  1. 00:00:00

    Cactus Quest returns for a greenhouse visit

    Host Hunter Still introduces a 2024 tour of Mike’s collection. Unlike a production nursery, the space reflects a collector who stages many species and objects together.

  2. 00:00:28

    Mike’s collection extends beyond Astrophytum

    Hunter distinguishes the greenhouse from another recent tour: the structure is similar, but Mike has a wider range, many finished stagings, and a supporter’s relationship to Cactus Quest.

  3. 00:00:56

    The host locates his own work inside the ecosystem

    Before arriving, Hunter explains that videos, pottery, shirts, hats, plants, and seedlings support his full-time practice. Viewer purchases and direct support fund travel and more ambitious documentation.

  4. 00:01:52

    Four years of repeated work became a living

    Hunter realizes that the activity he hoped might become a career has already occupied several years. The episode then moves from creator support into Mike’s greenhouse.

  5. 00:02:49

    A larger cedar greenhouse changes everyday use

    Mike replaced a smaller structure that forced him to crouch with an eight-by-nine-foot Costco greenhouse. More headroom, better material, temporary lights, and the ability to sit through rain make it a place rather than only storage.

  6. 00:03:47

    Fast assembly created repair work

    Impatient with the installer’s two-week schedule, Mike hired independent workers and paid more than expected. The roof then leaked, requiring resealing and extra ventilation after heavy rain.

  7. 00:04:16

    The new environment is being learned cautiously

    Because the space is young, Mike does not yet know how humidity and airflow will affect fungi or plant health. He avoids adding unnecessary water while watching how the collection responds.

  8. 00:04:44

    Gravel is chosen for sensory experience as well as function

    The decomposed-granite floor may retain moisture, but Mike likes its color, sound, and feel. Pots by several makers are stored underneath, making the room a combined plant and ceramic archive.

  9. 00:05:12

    Don Hunt pots preserve a maker who has died

    Hunter identifies multiple works by respected potter Don Hunt. Because no new pieces can be made, the vessels carry personal and community history beyond their role as containers.

  10. 00:05:40

    Staging begins with matching plant and vessel

    Mike credits Robert for teaching him to see how a caudiciform’s shape, pot, and eventual exposure belong together. Some plants require years of waiting before the hidden structure can be raised safely.

  11. 00:06:08

    Patience protects future form

    A plant that looks ordinary above soil may contain the main visual structure underground. Raising it too early can damage development, so staging becomes a long sequence rather than one reveal.

  12. 00:06:36

    Gradual exposure creates a bonsai-like practice

    The plant is lifted over several years while its roots and caudex adapt. Mike connects that controlled reveal with his growing interest in bonsai, habitat staging, and leaving nursery plastic behind.

  13. 00:07:04

    Top dressing brings another design language

    Special surface sand and habitat-style soil make the staging resemble a place rather than a retail pot. Mike recognizes his attraction to Japanese tools and labels, but appreciates that the plant community shares technique more openly than streetwear.

  14. 00:07:34

    The plant community accelerated a beginner’s learning

    Mike admits entering quickly without much knowledge. Hunter and other collectors answered questions rather than guarding expertise, allowing enthusiasm to become a more responsible practice.

  15. 00:08:03

    Playful ceramic stacking carries some risk

    Two Don Hunt pieces are combined into a dramatic pedestal, while another broken collaboration pot is salvaged instead of discarded. Repair and imperfect use are accepted parts of the collection.

  16. 00:09:01

    Darren Romanelli opened the collecting path

    A few months before the pandemic, Mike encountered Romanelli’s broad collection of art, ceramics, objects, and plants. That range suggested a living collection could connect his existing interests.

  17. 00:09:29

    Plants require care that rugs and objects do not

    Mike had accumulated rugs and obscure art without daily responsibility. A plant creates a different relationship because ownership includes water, environment, change, and the possibility of loss.

  18. 00:09:58

    Ariocarpus supplied the first alien attraction

    Living-rock forms, cauliflower textures, and unusual greens made the genus look unlike ordinary plants. Seeing different specimens reveal radically different growth taught Mike how much variation exists inside one name.

  19. 00:10:26

    Collection becomes a permanent learning loop

    Once small differences become visible, expertise no longer feels like a finish line. An old Euphorbia from Gary Lyons’ estate, recovering from near abandonment, adds history and rehabilitation to the aesthetic interest.

  20. 00:11:25

    Corking can become a desired visual trait

    Some collectors seek Obesa plants whose side corking creates a star-like top. The conversation shows how a condition one audience might reject can become a named, valued characteristic inside another community.

  21. 00:11:54

    Failed schoolwork gains meaning inside a design career

    Mike displays a typography test graded C-minus and a childhood statement predicting athletic halls of fame, wealth, and a large family. Both are funny evidence of a future no one could read accurately at the time.

  22. 00:12:22

    Childhood certainty is preserved as emotional evidence

    The writing is naïve, but Mike values its belief in a good life built through family and effort. It sits near Daniel Johnston, Grateful Dead work, a portrait by Ashley, and other personal art.

  23. 00:12:52

    One-of-one art bridges graphic work and collecting

    As a graphic designer, Mike produces reproducible images on shirts. Owning a singular work made by another person provides a different relationship to authorship and presence.

  24. 00:13:20

    Plants changed the reason he buys art

    Because plants can die under poor care, they taught Mike to ask whether he genuinely loves an object. A profitable acquisition is not worthwhile if it will remain hidden and emotionally unused.

  25. 00:13:48

    He refuses a valuable painting he does not like

    Mike was offered a work below market value and could imagine a large return. He declined because it would live in storage, making him the wrong custodian despite the financial opportunity.

  26. 00:14:18

    A good plant is the one its owner wants to see

    Hunter tells buyers that he cannot certify a universally correct specimen. Satisfaction, curiosity, and willingness to care for it matter more than copying the canonical item every collector already owns.

  27. 00:14:46

    Status collecting has to be separated from curiosity

    Mike admits initially chasing large, attention-generating specimens. Over time, learning a genus and caring for its differences became more rewarding than displaying one universally recognized grail.

  28. 00:15:14

    Care knowledge gives the collection credibility

    A rare plant without husbandry skill is like a closet without personal style. Mike recognizes that he moved too quickly and now calls more experienced growers to repair gaps in his practice.

  29. 00:16:11

    Shared technique reduces ego

    Plant growers often explain methods that streetwear operators would call secret sauce. Hunter describes this generosity as one reason the community feels less performative and more centered on something living outside the self.

  30. 00:16:39

    A failed Ariocarpus rescue still taught the network

    Hunter recounts panicking over an expensive plant that declined before its first watering. An experienced Tucson grower advised him, but the plant died, demonstrating that help does not guarantee control.

  31. 00:17:36

    Thirty-five years of care cannot be priced only as material

    An old specimen contains the accumulated work of previous owners. Trades and temporary care arrangements are therefore built on trust that the next person will preserve more than resale value.

  32. 00:18:33

    The contrast with streetwear remains explicit

    Mike returns to the difference between guarded fashion knowledge and growers who open doors. The plant world has its difficult personalities, but most people he meets are willing to speak.

  33. 00:19:01

    Small creators can feel more relatable than famous experts

    Mike values collectors whose music, incense, Pokémon figures, and backyard setups give each greenhouse character. Their scale is attainable enough to show a believable path of growth.

  34. 00:19:29

    Tennis YouTube offers the same accessible middle

    He follows obscure tennis creators who are not elite players because their improvement is easier to imagine. A giant professional nursery can inspire awe, but a serious backyard practice demonstrates a next step.

  35. 00:19:58

    Experienced growers become cultural historians

    Visits with Woody and other respected figures feel like meeting celebrity craftspeople, but their reputations grew from decades of hobby knowledge and love rather than mass visibility.

  36. 00:20:26

    Plant geometry is living art with an obligation

    Symmetry and desert form make the specimens visually complete, but the collector must keep them alive. Mike points to a damaged plant that grew around a stone, incorporating an accident into its future shape.

  37. 00:21:24

    Daily change separates plants from static archives

    A book or coin collection can remain inert, while growth gives Mike a reason to return each day. Astrophytum species and cultivated Japanese forms show extreme variation within one biological family.

  38. 00:21:53

    Wild species and selected cultivars require different reading

    Hunter explains that one star cactus resembles what could be found in Texas or Mexico, while another is the result of generations of cultivation and hybrid selection.

  39. 00:22:23

    Cultivation behaves like collaborative art across generations

    The host compares crossing selected plants with combining the visual languages of major painters. A grower finds a rare trait, preserves the mother, and develops a lineage no single desert specimen would provide.

  40. 00:23:20

    Pottery adds a second evolving collection

    Large commissioned vessels wait for future plants, while lower staging pots are designed to recede and frame the specimen. Plant collecting led Mike toward a separate love of ceramics.

  41. 00:24:17

    Ashley’s ceramic cactus gives dead spines a second life

    Mike’s girlfriend sculpted a Melocactus form and inserted real spines saved from a dying plant. The object blurs specimen, memorial, and sculpture while visually disappearing among living plants.

  42. 00:25:15

    The sculpture became the beginning of a series

    Studio visitors were surprised by the painstaking construction and danger of handling real spines. Mike places it inside the greenhouse as both artwork and continuation of a lost cactus.

  43. 00:25:44

    Mike admits collecting before learning to grow

    He began with Ariocarpus, spread quickly into unfamiliar species, and now adds smaller plants he expects to develop alongside over ten or fifteen years.

  44. 00:26:14

    Estate plants are preserved even when visually rough

    A neglected Gary Lyons specimen may look unattractive, but its history and recovery matter to Mike. Other gifted plants show small signs of new growth that make care feel active.

  45. 00:26:42

    Staging remains an unfinished skill

    Mike experiments with rocks, angle, and pot placement while admitting breakage and weak decisions. The greenhouse is presented as a learning space, not a finished expert display.

  46. 00:27:11

    Pruned Dyckia and trained Adenium become new interests

    Plants Mike initially did not understand become desirable after someone reveals their trunk or root architecture. Thai growers train young roots around supports, making decades of intervention visible.

  47. 00:28:08

    Rats introduce an ethical care conflict

    Rodents damaged a rare plant, so Mike hired a pest specialist. He loves animals but recognizes that protecting the collection requires choices he does not feel entirely comfortable making.

  48. 00:29:06

    Gary Lyons’ front-yard specimens await a permanent landscape

    Mike and John Trager excavated old Euphorbia, Agave, and other plants after Lyons’ death. The greenhouse is temporary shelter until heavy specimens can be planted in the ground.

  49. 00:30:02

    Mutation and ordinary accessibility coexist

    A crested or triangular growth can be visually extraordinary, while many fat caudiciforms remain obtainable to beginners. The conversation encourages looking in overlooked nursery corners rather than assuming every compelling plant is expensive.

  50. 00:30:58

    Unpotting becomes a delayed reveal

    A buried caudex may emerge with unexpected folds and direction. Replanting at an angle lets the collector compose with that form rather than restoring the exact orientation in which it grew.

  51. 00:31:26

    Natural-looking staging uses support deliberately

    A rock can hold a leaning plant and make the composition feel discovered rather than arranged. Mike points to a damaged specimen returning to growth as an object he values more because recovery remains visible.

  52. 00:32:22

    Resilience creates plant devotion

    Hunter remembers a cactus surviving months upside down and partially buried. Its continued growth made the organism’s ability to heal emotionally legible rather than merely botanical.

  53. 00:32:53

    Old locality tags preserve lost documentation habits

    A Huntington specimen from Lyons’ collection still carries precise origin information. The hosts note that such detailed tags would be unusual on ordinary sale plants today.

  54. 00:33:21

    A deteriorating grow bag still supports new pups

    Another old Euphorbia survives in a bag whose sides have broken away, benefiting incidentally from airflow and drainage. Mike plans to move more of these temporary survivors into stable ground.

  55. 00:33:49

    The tour closes without treating size as value

    Hunter thanks Mike for opening a relatively small greenhouse. Both emphasize that a meaningful collection comes from care, history, and relationships rather than square footage or specimen count.
