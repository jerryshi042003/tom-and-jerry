Full interview, in order

  1. 00:00:00

    The whole team introduces the brand

    Moderator Waleed Mohammed asks the Chinatown Market staff to identify their roles across design, production, fulfillment, warehousing, marketing, and creative direction. The panel is framed as a collective account of how the company began, operates, and hopes to grow.

  2. 00:01:08

    A desperate ComplexCon experiment became the opening

    Mike recalls losing ICNY, moving to California, and arriving at ComplexCon in 2016 with a free booth and only a handful of shirts and hats. A topical Frank Ocean graphic drew attention and gave him evidence that a new brand could form around fast, playful ideas.

  3. 00:02:56

    Dylan turned persistent outreach into a job

    While finishing apparel studies in Indiana, Dylan repeatedly sent Mike designs. One sold out, so he moved to Los Angeles, lived in a hostel, and took on whatever the young operation needed before growing into marketing and design leadership.

  4. 00:05:04

    Willingness to fail matters more than a conventional résumé

    Mike uses Dylan's story to explain his hiring instinct: he looks for energy, initiative, and excitement rather than a prescribed background. The team treats making clothes as a privilege and wants colleagues who participate with the same urgency.

  5. 00:07:01

    Each employee reached streetwear through a different door

    The panelists trace their interests to Tumblr outfits, skate shops, garment durability, social confidence, older siblings, and early experiments. Mike's path began with fashion-industry parents and a childhood desire to make a product of his own.

  6. 00:09:21

    Early failure makes the current scale intelligible

    Mike describes ordering hundreds of pieces for an early brand and eventually unloading them to a secondhand shop when buyers never appeared. The lesson is to begin with one object and one customer rather than confusing inventory with demand.

  7. 00:10:24

    Customization makes the office process public

    Chinatown Market University is designed to show customers that products emerge from ordinary collaboration: one person proposes an idea, others alter it, and the team produces it. Participation is meant to make creation feel attainable even as a hobby.

  8. 00:12:21

    The weekly Maker Shirt program removes gatekeeping

    On Instagram Live, viewers can talk directly with the staff, design a shirt together, and receive the result free. The team presents this access as gratitude to the audience and as proof that followers or insider status are not prerequisites for making something.

  9. 00:14:37

    A brand account should feel like people speaking

    The marketing team answers comments and messages in an informal voice because the account is genuinely run by the people seen in the office. Mike says the audience helped build the company, so the relationship should not resemble a distant corporation broadcasting at consumers.

  10. 00:15:56

    Collaborations are useful when they carry a specific challenge

    Mike rejects the idea that a famous partner automatically makes a project meaningful. Work with Puma and other large companies is valuable when it lets MARKET enter a new community, interpret a partner's history, or make an unfamiliar audience feel included.

  11. 00:17:28

    Streetwear can challenge fashion's artificial distance

    The discussion treats runway fashion and streetwear as related creative platforms separated largely by price and access. Mike wants young creators to see that prestigious spaces are not closed by nature and that independent brands can force the category to explain itself.

  12. 00:18:21

    The long-term product is a community, not a T-shirt

    For the next year, Mike prioritizes continued connection, humor, and inspiration over a rigid collection plan. Trends move too quickly for merchandise alone to sustain meaning, while a community can remember how the brand made participation feel.

  13. 00:19:53

    In-house speed lets the team answer culture while it is alive

    The staff explains its quick-strike workflow: notice an internet moment, design and photograph a response, list only the demand, then print and ship immediately. Tight coordination makes the product arrive before the joke has become stale.

  14. 00:21:38

    The smiley face and Chinatown name came from one hour of riffing

    In audience questions, Mike explains that the identity emerged quickly with a friend, but the smiley grew into a durable statement of optimism. He wants the company to uplift people rather than treat them only as purchasers.

  15. 00:23:12

    Growth creates pressure to predict the next thing

    Mike names the hardest part of expansion as balancing play with the obligations of a real business. Creativity becomes exhausting when everyone asks what comes next, so he distinguishes a private hobby from a company that must listen to and serve other people.

  16. 00:24:45

    Gratitude protects the original ambition

    Asked what still motivates him, Mike returns to the impossible-seeming dream that began the work and to the many people who would value the same opportunity. Dylan adds that a more inclusive streetwear landscape now gives unfamiliar small brands a credible route forward.

  17. 00:26:18

    Made-to-order production and reuse answer waste

    On sustainability, the team says quick strikes are produced against actual sales instead of speculative piles. They also describe an atelier program in which employees repurpose vintage goods, surplus hoodies, and other existing material into one-off designs.

  18. 00:29:08

    Attention is less rewarding than making alongside people

    Mike admits that public visibility can become awkward, but says the enjoyable part remains meeting customers and creating with the team. Colleagues describe him working directly at events rather than separating himself as the founder.

  19. 00:30:53

    Overseas production should follow proven local demand

    For a question about scaling manufacturing, Mike advises keeping early production close enough to control and learn. More complex overseas products become sensible only after a community and retail demand exist, and even broad distribution can introduce the work to people the founder could not reach alone.

  20. 00:32:58

    The panel closes where the operating philosophy began

    Mike acknowledges that some peers called early wholesale distribution a sellout move, but sees accessible stores as discovery channels. His final advice is to start small, advance one step at a time, and join the team's hands-on customization program at the event.
