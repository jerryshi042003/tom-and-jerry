Exposing Chinatown Market's Biggest Secret (Mike Cherman)

Superline · 2024-02-09 · 00:26:20 · interview

Summary

  • What this is: A compact career interview about selling shirts in school, losing control of work made for others, choosing accessibility over exclusivity, and changing the Chinatown Market name.
  • Context: A 00:26:20 interview published by Superline on 2024-02-09.
  • Selling one shirt exposed the calling. A school controversy around a homemade shirt made Mike realize product could create a social reaction.
  • Unprotected development can be taken. He recounts building samples for a designer who left with the work after securing a larger deal.
  • Access matters more than resale status. Mike frames ICNY and MARKET as products meant to reach people, not only signal membership in a tiny scene.
  • The name had to change. He traces the casual origin of Chinatown Market and why the later responsibility could not be avoided.

Transcript summary

  1. The opening montage identifies the recurring themes

    The episode begins with Mike’s first meaningful shirt, the sudden Frank Ocean sales spike, products that create joy, access rather than elite aspiration, and the later decision to remove Chinatown from the company name.

  2. Mikey Merchandise came from a school conflict

    Mike explains that he was punished for selling shirts on campus after a student fought over a design. The nickname followed, but more importantly the event showed him that a product he made could provoke a real social response.

  3. The incident revealed a possible life’s work

    Being called to the principal’s office because of a shirt felt like proof that making mattered. Mike connects that moment to deciding he wanted to keep making T-shirts for the rest of his life.

  4. A product can package an emotion

    The host asks whether this was the first time Mike saw his work affect someone. Mike compares it with the childhood feeling that new basketball shoes make you faster: design can attach confidence, excitement, or identity to an object.

  5. Emotional response is a design achievement

    Mike says the highest accomplishment is not merely making something visually cool. It is creating a product that changes how its owner feels and continues carrying that feeling after the first purchase.

  6. The smiley basketball is designed to perform happiness

    A familiar ball becomes memorable because the smiling object produces an immediate reaction. Mike contrasts that success with the many early designs that generated no meaningful response.

  7. Early work openly imitated admired designers

    Mike recalls a logo shirt and silhouette graphics influenced by Full Bleed, Johnny Cupcakes, and the Myspace-era independent brand world. Copying the structure of work he loved was part of learning how graphics were made.

  8. Self-teaching moved from software to physical shirts

    After teaching himself Adobe tools, Mike bought transfer paper at Office Depot and printed garments in the garage. His parents had fashion backgrounds, but he had dismissed their women’s-wear work when he was younger.

  9. His parents supplied complementary instincts

    Mike now recognizes his mother’s creative influence and his father’s sales ability in his own practice. Although both warned him about the industry, they can see how their skills were recombined in his career.

  10. A personal voice emerged through proximity and repetition

    Mike says every designer begins by referencing what they know. His voice changed after he encountered real production, demanding customers, mentors, and high-level product rather than only remaking images from a distance.

  11. Goodwood taught custom translation

    At Goodwood, Mike turned emailed requests into mocked-up, laser-cut wooden chains for musicians and other customers. Making a stranger’s idea by the next day taught speed, communication, and how personalized product circulates.

  12. Getting fired led to a better apprenticeship

    Mike made extra chains for Chase Infinite, lost the shop job, and then interned for him. That brought him into a basement scene around emerging artists and operators whose relationships continued shaping his career.

  13. High-quality objects reset his standards

    Another mentor exposed Mike to Japanese denim and carefully executed product. Seeing the difference in person made material, finish, and construction legible in a way that images alone had not.

  14. Enter a room by becoming useful

    Mike advises young people to notice work someone is trying to accomplish and offer a concrete capability. Direct service to a mentor can create a reciprocal relationship more effectively than an internship chosen only for its name.

  15. Opportunity follows visible contribution

    Not every mentor will reciprocate, but Mike’s strongest relationships began because he repeatedly helped and made himself available. Those mentors introduced him to people he still works with years later.

  16. Consistency is required because access is crowded

    Many young people want the same creative roles. Mike says a person has to keep asking, working, and placing themselves in relevant environments even when the first attempts receive no response.

  17. Not every business relationship returns the favor

    The host asks about exploitative experiences. Mike distinguishes generally supportive mentors from occasional commercial arrangements where his labor produced less recognition or continued work than he expected.

  18. A development job disappeared after the client’s deal

    Mike describes helping a prominent designer produce hats and samples. Once that designer secured a larger partnership, the relationship ended and similar finished products appeared through the new company.

  19. Vendor work does not automatically create authorship

    Although the experience felt unfair, Mike corrects the host’s claim that his work was stolen. He had developed someone else’s creative direction as a vendor, which clarified the need to understand one’s contractual role and expectations.

  20. Bootlegging is aimed upward, not sideways

    Mike describes a Robin Hood rule: play with the codes of giant luxury and global businesses rather than copying another small neighboring brand. The distinction is ethical intent, scale, and whether the new object adds a point of view.

  21. An unofficial Grateful Dead shirt became licensed work

    The rights holder contacted MARKET and proposed working legally. That conversation expanded into a Crocs partnership and a sustained licensed program rather than ending with one cease-and-desist moment.

  22. Timing turned a license into a cultural wave

    MARKET’s Grateful Dead work arrived alongside renewed mainstream attention and other major collaborations. Mike sees a strong collaboration as contributing a distinct product to a moment already forming across music and fashion.

  23. Creative enthusiasm has to be sold internally

    In corporate partnership meetings, a compelling design is only half the job. Mike has learned to translate excitement into a reason, a business case, and a presentation that decision-makers can understand.

  24. A disruptive idea often needs repeated explanation

    Some partners immediately see an opportunity and others do not. MARKET makes decks, visual concepts, and repeated pitches so that an unfamiliar proposal becomes concrete enough for a large company to approve.

  25. Communication is a permanent part of collaboration

    Mike rejects the fantasy that a creative can simply demand freedom. Selling the premise to non-specialists is an ongoing operating skill, especially when the requested work falls outside a partner’s normal plan.

  26. A brand needs fireworks and a continuing fire

    Not every product should be a viral provocation. MARKET uses occasional high-attention collaborations to bring people into a broader seasonal business of graphics, familiar products, and ordinary releases.

  27. Spectacle supports the dependable assortment

    A strange Crocs hybrid or other headline object refreshes attention. Once people enter through that moment, they may choose a smiley shirt or another simple product that keeps the company operating between spectacles.

  28. The content voice changes with audience behavior

    Earlier, Mike handed the phone to many employees and built recognizable office characters. After the pandemic, audiences returned to physical life and the fantasy of watching an inaccessible office culture became less central.

  29. Fashion and media targets keep moving

    Changes in dress, shopping, mobility, and social habits force the company to reconsider what participation looks like. A content system that worked while people stayed home cannot simply be preserved as mythology.

  30. A useful gap may not look hyper-cool

    Mike identifies a widespread mistake: assuming that respect requires serving the smallest, most exclusive niche. A real business can come from recognizing an ordinary group with an unmet need and serving it well.

  31. The smiley ball says you can participate too

    Elite sports marketing speaks as though every buyer should become exceptional. MARKET’s alternative is an object for fans, beginners, parents, and children who may never play professionally but can still love the game.

  32. Fun is an underserved sports need

    Mike imagines a parent choosing equipment that helps a child enjoy activity rather than measuring worth only through achievement. He wants the brand to validate participation without pretending everyone will become elite.

  33. Accessibility was present before MARKET

    The host asks how Mike matured beyond wanting the coolest brand. Mike says exclusivity was never fully his model: even ICNY began by making safety product for ordinary cyclists rather than producing a rare status object.

  34. Broad availability is not a late compromise

    MARKET products have generally remained obtainable rather than reselling at extreme multiples. Mike connects that choice with wanting many people to experience the work, even while admiring expensive technical brands himself.

  35. Technical performance exposed a harder comparison

    With ICNY, Mike had to compete directly against established outdoor garments on construction, protection, and price. Graphic products offer a faster and more flexible way to create emotion, but also face a constantly shifting cultural target.

  36. A moving audience can be creatively useful

    Rather than serving one fixed group of technical-jacket buyers, MARKET moves among large and small cultural moments. Mike finds the instability interesting because each release tests a different kind of connection.

  37. Voice begins with what genuinely excites the maker

    To an eighteen-year-old still searching for style, Mike recommends looking inward before adding visual polish. If the subject does not animate the maker, audiences will quickly sense that the product lacks purpose.

  38. Finding a voice still requires surviving criticism

    Mike recalls ordinary jobs and the luck involved in making clothes professionally. Passion becomes credible only through waking up and continuing while people dismiss the product or say the work is not good enough.

  39. Repetition makes an object recognizable

    The same shirt had to appear repeatedly before it became an icon, and the smiley basketball looked disruptive only after the company kept showing it in unexpected contexts such as Puma’s basketball launch.

  40. Early fulfillment chaos felt like proof

    Mike remembers phones vibrating with orders, printing long runs of shipping labels, filming them across the floor, and inviting friends to pack. The intensity was exhausting but supplied a visible link between an idea and people wanting it.

  41. Purpose appears where excitement and service overlap

    He compares the rush to gambling, but values that the energy can improve his own life and make another person’s experience easier or happier. A durable business sits at that intersection.

  42. Being copied is part of the system he used

    Asked how he feels when major companies adopt MARKET-like codes, Mike says he cannot condemn the same circulation that made bootlegging possible for him. He would prefer an official call, but treats shared visual language as part of the game.

  43. A possible Nike imitation became a relationship choice

    After a Nike designer bought ICNY socks, a similar reflective motif appeared on a Kevin Durant product. Mike could have closed the door, but instead continued inviting Nike teams to visit and understand how his company works.

  44. Open doors preserve future collaboration

    Mike would rather share the studio’s thinking and remain available when a large company is ready to work than turn one grievance into permanent isolation. The host identifies this as the sales instinct balancing his creative side.

  45. Human connection multiplies opportunity

    Talent without public contact remains invisible. Mike treats visits, conversation, and generosity as the conditions through which future work becomes possible, even when no immediate deal is attached.

  46. The Chinatown name began in an unexamined rush

    The host asks directly about the rename. Mike recounts riffing from Canal Street shirts and bag graphics until a ten-design mini-collection existed, without pausing to examine what the location’s name represented.

  47. A free ComplexCon booth forced a decision

    A Shopify contact offered space at the first ComplexCon just as Mike watched friends from Pleasures take their young brand abroad. Frustration with standing still pushed him to finance five shirts and five hats and show up.

  48. The launch combined urgency, debt, and infringement

    After the booth, Mike bought a Frank Ocean-related domain from a U-Haul and generated rapid online sales, then refunded the money after an infringement notice. The episode proved both the demand and the legal weakness of the first idea.

  49. ComplexCon restored confidence after ICNY

    Mike had put roughly twenty thousand dollars on a credit card after losing his former company. Earning that amount during the first day suggested he could create demand again and survive the restart.

  50. Cash made the abstract recovery visible

    Back at the hotel, Mike and his girlfriend spread out the cash from the booth and sat with the scale of the response. Early orders from Colette and Urban Outfitters reinforced the feeling that the new project had crossed into a real market.

  51. Access survived accusations of selling out

    Some peers predicted the brand would die because it entered broad retail early. Mike argues that opening the door matters; if a company chooses scarcity, the smaller experience has to be unusually rich to justify exclusion.

  52. Small design details create durable memory

    He recalls hidden pockets and unexpected zippers from products he loved as a child. Objects are materially ordinary, but a carefully designed surprise can produce joy and support a long relationship with a brand.

  53. The advice to his younger self is consistency with enjoyment

    Milestones become new versions of old challenges, so delaying all satisfaction until success wastes the most alive part of building. Mike recommends keeping going while also noticing the fun inside the unfinished period.

  54. Family impact gives the business proportion

    Helping his parents and buying his mother something she still enjoys makes the work feel consequential. At the same time, Mike reminds himself that he makes shirts, graphic balls, and novelty objects rather than claiming to save the world.

  55. The final goal is modest and direct

    Mike acknowledges the contradiction between producing more goods and talking abstractly about sustainability. His closing standard is simpler: make something that causes a genuine smile and remain grateful for the chance to keep creating.
