  1. 00:00:00

    Sean Wotherspoon enters Mike’s archive

    Sean introduces Mike as a designer who pushes the field forward, then arrives at the downtown storage space for an object-by-object conversation rather than a conventional career interview.

  2. 00:00:29

    The archive mixes brand history with personal collecting

    Mike describes shirts, rugs, sneakers, and company artifacts in one room. The first shelf is a tennis group containing found vintage pieces and special garments made around current players.

  3. 00:00:57

    Tennis shirts record people and gatherings

    A one-of-one Psyduck homage was made for Taylor Townsend, while another shirt came from a friends’ tournament organized around neighborhood teams. Sean says he sends Mike vintage tennis discoveries whenever he finds them.

  4. 00:01:25

    Hand-painted Michael Rios pieces preserve visible labor

    Mike recalls visiting the artist’s San Francisco studio during a Jimi Hendrix Estate collaboration. Screen printing establishes the main image and hand painting adds individual background color, making each piece visibly specific.

  5. 00:01:54

    Music shirts and jokes share the same shelf

    The conversation moves through Hendrix, Dave Matthews Band, and other vintage graphics. The archive is not arranged as a strict museum; cultural jokes, personal memory, and design influence sit beside one another.

  6. 00:02:22

    A Rogue Status shirt revealed reflective material

    Mike identifies a fully reflective shirt as one of the most important objects from his childhood. A flash photograph made the whole graphic illuminate and introduced the visual possibility later central to ICNY.

  7. 00:02:51

    Reflective apparel belonged to an early-2000s visual moment

    Sean connects the shirt to an era of 3M-heavy streetwear. Mike then uses the material thread to move from childhood inspiration into ICNY and later reflective MARKET products.

  8. 00:03:20

    ICNY turned the visual effect into a safety system

    After dropping out and working at Nike, Mike began making reflective socks following a cycling collision. The material was not only impressive under flash; it helped make a moving rider visible at night.

  9. 00:03:48

    A lost brand does not end the ability to build

    Mike reminds viewers that Chinatown Market was not his first attempt. Having started several projects since ICNY, he treats a brand’s loss as painful but not as proof that the underlying making ability disappeared.

  10. 00:04:18

    The hat wall records niche brands and personal silhouette

    Vintage caps, technical shapes, and a Romanelli/Fair collaboration lead into Mike’s preference for shallow, performance-influenced hats. Sean recognizes that silhouette as part of Mike’s personal uniform.

  11. 00:04:46

    Tennis hats and a worn Snoop piece carry use

    The collection includes Tennis Is Life, Penn, and racket-sport hats alongside an original Doggy Style cap Mike wore heavily. Wear is treated as evidence of attachment rather than a reason to exclude the object.

  12. 00:05:14

    Odd sports and brand combinations become research

    An ATP Tour watch-company cap, Herschel work, and early ICNY hats reveal how unrelated logos, typography, and technical blanks can suggest new combinations later.

  13. 00:05:44

    Small construction details date the object

    A 2010-era ICNY hat uses a stretch Flexfit base with a snap closure. The men notice the contradiction because the blank itself captures a particular period of performance materials entering casual headwear.

  14. 00:06:12

    ICNY referenced earlier revival brands

    Mike points to a hat influenced by Brooklyn Basement and Ralph Lauren Sportsman work. His own project participated in a next generation of Polo, rugby, and outdoor-reference revival rather than inventing that language alone.

  15. 00:06:42

    Technical classics remain personal anchors

    Arc’teryx bucket hats and memories of Snow Beach and P-Wing revivals connect the archive to products Mike still considers exceptionally resolved. The discussion keeps returning from market value to why a shape works.

  16. 00:07:11

    A strange jacket matters even when it sold poorly

    A transformable, pillow-like Chinatown Market jacket looks irrational as inventory. Mike and Sean argue that such objects established the company’s character because they made people ask why the product existed.

  17. 00:07:39

    The pirate-ship energy can be lost during scale

    Irreverent applications distinguished the young brand, but a larger company can forget the experimentation that created demand. A hand-painted fan piece reminds Mike of direct participation around the early smiley graphic.

  18. 00:08:07

    Round Two witnessed the Los Angeles restart

    Sean recalls meeting Mike through Alex James shortly after Mike moved from New York. Within about a year, Mike announced Chinatown Market and translated downtown references into the first ComplexCon booth.

  19. 00:08:35

    The launch identity remained legible over time

    Neon, Thank You bags, smiley faces, and New York retail codes created a coherent booth that still connects to products the company prints years later. The smiley became a commercial engine without losing its simple visual premise.

  20. 00:09:05

    Selling personal grails financed a new life

    Mike arrived in Los Angeles with debt but with valuable sneakers, Supreme pieces, and a Jordan flight suit. Round Two paid roughly seven thousand dollars, covering rent, a car, and the ability to keep attempting a new brand.

  21. 00:09:34

    Guest Farmers Market gathered a future community

    Sean curated an early platform for Chinatown Market, Pleasures, Fontaine Cards, Utmost, and other young projects. The point was not that they were already large, but that the group had the energy to become important together.

  22. 00:10:02

    A platform can be valuable before the brands are proven

    Sean remembers promising Guess that the participating labels would grow. Years later, the memory demonstrates how an established company can provide space without demanding that every participant already possess scale.

  23. 00:10:31

    A canceled Tommy project survives through samples

    Mike shows elaborate cycling jerseys, sweatsuits, flags, and heavily embellished pieces from a collaboration that never launched. The garments document production ambition even though the public never received the collection.

  24. 00:10:59

    The vintage references reveal the production burden

    Original Tommy Hilfiger pieces with puffers, mesh overlays, and complex construction sit beside the samples. Seeing both makes clear that translating a reference faithfully is expensive and technically demanding.

  25. 00:11:27

    Sourcing and relationships fed the Tommy study

    Some vintage came from dealers or stores, and Sean recalls Tommy buying large amounts of archive material from Round Two. Mike also had an earlier connection to Tommy’s son through album-cover work.

  26. 00:11:55

    A strong prototype still needs organizational support

    The men admire the collection’s clear materials and execution, then note that the relationship collapsed. The archive preserves what the partner’s public release system failed to carry.

  27. 00:12:23

    A mentor’s jacket turns history into a test

    Chase Infinite gave Mike a Prohibit jacket mapping sites where revolutionaries were killed. Chase would quiz him on every reference, using clothing as a prompt to learn rather than merely as a rare object.

  28. 00:12:51

    A Nike-space piece with the Ice Cold mark and matching tattoo connects current Mike to the earliest identity. Capital pants and painted vintage garments then extend the shelf into other makers’ transformation methods.

  29. 00:13:19

    Ace Face pants use old graphics addressing racism, music, Supreme, and other cultural fragments. The object shows how an archive can be recomposed into one garment while keeping its source images visible.

  30. 00:13:48

    Sean’s hiking pants were a development lesson

    Sean brought Mike an altered vintage hiking pant, and Mike helped convert it into a reproducible new product. Sean calls it a north star because it was his first experience turning a found object into modern production.

  31. 00:14:16

    Rapid growth encouraged collaborative improvisation

    As both businesses accelerated, Mike entered Sean’s project asking how he could help before feeling fully prepared. The memory values repeated studio sessions and shared problem solving more than a formal service arrangement.

  32. 00:14:44

    Puma and Gyakusou keep the technical line visible

    An early reflective Puma/ICNY jacket and modular, taped, lightweight Nike Gyakusou pieces illustrate Mike’s long attachment to performance construction. The shelf makes the through-line from cycling safety to fashion legible.

  33. 00:15:12

    One profitable rug purchase created a collecting spiral

    Mike bought a rug, had it cleaned, and received a surprisingly high offer. That success encouraged broad rug collecting, although he is careful to say the financial luck did not repeat automatically.

  34. 00:15:41

    War rugs and contemporary art rugs carry narrative

    The collection includes historical conflict imagery and a newer piece about Colombia’s Banana Wars. Mike is drawn to rugs as surfaces that can hold politics, memory, and graphic composition simultaneously.

  35. 00:16:09

    Rug technique moved into footwear

    MARKET’s hotel slipper applied rug material to an everyday shoe before monster-like textured footwear became common. The product looked extreme at the time because the material category and object category rarely met.

  36. 00:16:37

    A wooden briefcase became memorable bad inventory

    Sean proposed recreating a 1950s flooring-salesman case. Mike helped manufacture roughly five hundred, and the object sold so poorly that boxes followed Sean through multiple moves.

  37. 00:17:06

    Failure does not erase the object’s authorship

    The briefcase expresses Sean’s tendency to request something stranger than another shirt. Both men laugh at the financial decision while retaining pride in the historical reference and physical execution.

  38. 00:17:34

    A Jeff Hamilton collaboration collided with the rename

    One of the last major Chinatown Market projects included leather jackets, basketball products, shoes, and reflective smileys. The name controversy arrived during rollout, leading retailers to pull or off-price much of the work.

  39. 00:18:30

    Reflective material links the whole room

    Sean notices the sequence from the childhood shirt through ICNY and into elaborate MARKET footwear. Even commercially weak Timberland experiments continue the same interest in making a familiar object transform under light.

  40. 00:18:58

    A product can be early and still fail

    Shoes dismissed at release later resemble silhouettes popular in the current market. Mike does not turn that into proof of genius; being ahead of timing does not eliminate the inventory and support problem.

  41. 00:19:27

    A shoe-inside-a-shoe concept lacked the launch it needed

    An unreleased Puma sample layers a Vans-like slip-on and old-school upper, with clear and reflective versions. Mike argues that the idea needed partner support and narrative to become understandable at retail.

  42. 00:19:55

    The lab made techniques visible to visitors

    Flocking, debossed reflective work, heat pressing, and one-of-one shirts could be tested inside Chinatown Market. Sean remembers visiting because the lab allowed friends to participate in production rather than only seeing finished goods.

  43. 00:20:23

    UV Converse translated childhood effects at global scale

    Color-changing shoes became one of the company’s major commercial successes, selling in very large quantities. Mike connects UV and heat-reactive work to effects his generation already loved as children.

  44. 00:20:51

    An unreleased backpack shoe records another employee’s voice

    A heavy shoe with storage came from an early Blazy/Excel Arigato idea developed at Chinatown Market. Technical weight prevented release, but the sample documents the designer’s willingness to work late and pursue unusual concepts.

  45. 00:21:20

    Mike moves through éS-style shoes, early rhinestone sneakers, and later similarities elsewhere. He avoids claiming direct copying, using the timing instead to show how multiple designers can circle the same dormant code.

  46. 00:21:49

    Federer shorts are tennis history, not personal wardrobe

    Rare Nike Air Max/RF shorts were bought because Mike felt they belonged in a tennis archive, even though he does not intend to wear them. The object marks Federer’s earlier Nike identity before On.

  47. 00:22:18

    Crocs collaborations became major category artifacts

    Grass-textured and Grateful Dead Crocs sit among Hoka, ASICS, crystal Air Force 1s, and other footwear. The shelf treats mass-market novelty and technical shoes as equal evidence of how categories move.

  48. 00:23:15

    Some technically strong ICNY shoes lacked social distribution

    Reflective running shoes and ripstop models arrived before Instagram could effectively circulate their visual effect. Mike acknowledges strong influences while noting that product without a communication system rarely gains a life.

  49. 00:23:47

    A fan-made leather Swoosh Chuck preserves exchange

    During a China trip, a maker gave Mike a one-of-one vegetable-tanned version of MARKET’s unofficial shoe. It is difficult to wear but valuable as evidence that another person interpreted the brand through their own craft.

  50. 00:24:15

    A large China trip linked the Los Angeles scene

    Mike and Sean remember a plane filled with familiar LA operators, visits to bootleg malls, and ideas that circulated quickly among friends. Pleasures’ later bag project grew from discoveries on the same trip.

  51. 00:25:12

    Hand-pressed Swoosh Chucks expose their method

    Flocking peels and the overprinted smiley reveal that the shoes were made individually rather than manufactured as perfect factory objects. The worn construction is part of the artifact’s evidence.

  52. 00:25:41

    The archive ends where the career began

    The final shelf returns to ICNY shoes and reflective socks. After many collaborations and failed experiments, the earliest safety product remains the compact object from which Mike’s full brand history can be reconstructed.
